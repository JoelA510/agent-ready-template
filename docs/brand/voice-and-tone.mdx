# Voice & Tone

## Start with what's real

The organization named on these pages is a placeholder the template ships with — replace its
name, its tagline, and the voice described below with your own. The one piece of its copy
treated as "real" here is the tagline on the live site: **"We help teams do their best work."**
Everything below is a proposal for how to extend that one sentence into a full voice — not
a transcription of existing written guidance, because there isn't any yet. The starting
site is intentionally minimal: a tagline, a nav (Product / Customers / Pricing / Contact),
and the logo. There's no larger body of marketing copy to mine, and most teams adopting
this system start in exactly that position — one published line and nothing written down.

That constraint is actually a gift. It means the tagline gets to do more work than a
single sentence normally would — it's the clearest signal available for how Acme
wants to sound: warm without being casual, values-forward without being vague, and
credible without reading like a machine-generated document. Everything that follows is
built to sit comfortably next to that sentence, not to compete with it. Treat this page as
a strong starting recommendation your organization should push on, adjust, or override —
not a voice that's already locked in — and swap in your own tagline and attributes as the
real replacement for Acme's placeholders.

## Voice: who Acme always is

Voice doesn't change depending on where the words show up — that's tone's job, covered
further down. Voice is the constant personality underneath a marketing page, a billing
email, and an error message alike. Shopify's Polaris system is the model followed here:
name a small set of attributes, give each one a one-line definition, and show a real
do/don't pair rather than describing the attribute in the abstract. Four attributes are
proposed, chosen because Acme's copy spans two registers — a marketing-facing product
story and the plain, functional language of the app itself — and each attribute has to
hold up in both. These are Acme's placeholder attributes; keep the four-attribute
structure and replace the specifics with the personality your organization wants.

| Attribute | What it means | Do | Don't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clear** | Plain language over jargon by default. Reserve a technical term for when it's actually load-bearing — "cancel the subscription" can just be "cancel your plan" unless a precise distinction is at stake. | "We'll cancel your plan on the date you chose." | "The subscriber shall proceed to terminate the aforementioned subscription effective as of the date specified herein." |
| **Precise** | Say exactly what will happen and by when. Credibility rests on never being vague about consequences, especially the ones that cost a customer time or money. | "If we don't hear back by June 30, we'll archive this project — reopening it will need a new request." | "Please get back to us soon, or there could be some changes to your project status." |
| **Human** | Contractions are fine. "You" and "we" are fine. Match the warmth of the tagline rather than sounding like an automated system. | "We noticed your billing date moved — here's what changes for you." | "Notification: a modification to the previously scheduled billing date has been recorded in the system." |
| **Composed** | Even urgent or difficult news is delivered calmly and directly. Never alarmist, never buried under hedging. | "We moved your renewal to August 4. Here's what we need from you by July 28." | "URGENT!! Your renewal date has changed — please respond immediately!!" |

**An alternative worth naming:** a more traditional or enterprise-facing organization
might add a fifth attribute like *Authoritative* — leaning harder into formality as a
signal of gravitas. That's a real, defensible choice for a product selling on seriousness
and track record. It's not recommended for Acme's placeholder voice because it pulls
against a tagline about helping people "do their best work" rather than one about "the
industry standard" or "decades of experience." *Clear* and *Precise* already carry the
credibility that *Authoritative* would be trying to add, without the cost of sounding
distant. If your organization's read on this is different, that's a legitimate reason to
reopen the list.

## Tone: how voice flexes by context

Voice is the constant; tone is the situational adjustment on top of it. GOV.UK's content
guidance draws this line clearly and it's worth borrowing directly: the same voice
handles renewing a passport and reporting a death, but the tone in each case is obviously
not the same weight. Below are four contexts where Acme's tone should shift, with
the same four voice attributes underneath every one of them.

### Marketing site

Confident, warm, and focused on the reader's outcome — matching the energy of the tagline.
Still plain language, not hype-driven; Acme sells a considered product, not a countdown
timer.

- **Do:** "We help teams do their best work. Acme brings your projects, customers, and
  reporting into one place, so the people running the business can see it clearly."
- **Don't:** "🚀 We're revolutionizing the way changemakers get things done!"

### In-product transactional UI

Direct, efficient, minimal. The user is trying to get something done, not read prose.

- **Do:** "Project created."
- **Don't:** "Woohoo! Your project is ready to go!"

### Error / problem states

Calm, specific, and never a hint of blaming the person reading it — the error is the
system's problem to explain, not the user's fault to own.

- **Do:** "We couldn't save this document. Check your connection and try again."
- **Don't:** "You didn't enter this correctly." *(blames the user)* or "Something went
  wrong." *(too vague to act on)*

### Formal / compliance content

Terms of service, data-processing agreements, billing and subscription terms — anything
with legal weight. Precise and neutral — and this is the one context where it's worth
explicitly separating two things that look similar but aren't:

- **Required precision** — defined terms of art that carry real legal or contractual
  meaning and should stay exactly as-is: *personal data*, *service-level agreement (SLA)*,
  *sub-processor*. Simplifying these doesn't make them clearer, it makes them wrong.
- **Habitual jargon** — phrasing that's just inherited convention with no weight of its
  own: *pursuant to*, *hereinafter*, *the party of the first part*. These should default
  to plain language unless a specific drafting reason says otherwise.

- **Do:** "This agreement confirms the terms of your subscription to Acme. Your plan is
  billed monthly, in the amounts set out in Schedule A."
- **Don't:** "Pursuant to the terms hereinafter set forth, the undersigned provider
  (hereinafter 'Vendor') shall render services to the party of the first part."

## This isn't final

Everything on this page — the four attributes, the four contexts, the examples — is a
recommendation built around Acme's placeholder tagline, not a voice any real organization
has signed off on. Treat it as a starting proposal to adapt, and change what doesn't fit
once real people on your team weigh in and your own tagline replaces Acme's.
