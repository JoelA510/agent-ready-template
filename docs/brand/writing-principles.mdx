# Writing Principles

Voice & Tone (see the previous page) is about *who Acme sounds like*. This page is
about *how to write an individual sentence* — the mechanical habits that make copy easy
to read regardless of context. These are proposed as defaults for every writer touching
the system, from marketing copy to a button label, and — like the rest of this
section — they're a recommendation for your team to adjust, not a rule already carved in.

## Lead with the point

Put the most important information first. Readers — and especially customers scanning an
email between meetings — decide whether to keep reading in the first sentence. Burying
the point under context and caveats means the reader has to do the work of finding it.

- **Do:** "Your renewal has moved to August 4. We rescheduled it after a billing change;
  here's what changes for you."
- **Don't:** "Due to a change in your billing schedule, which was flagged to our team
  this morning, we wanted to let you know that there has been a change made to your
  renewal date, which is now August 4."

Both sentences contain the same facts. Only one lets the reader act on the first clause.

## Plain language over jargon — with a real carve-out

Default to the plainest word that says the same thing. Most formal-sounding phrasing is
habit, not requirement, and it costs the reader effort for no added precision.

| Jargon | Plain language |
| --- | --- |
| "prior to" | "before" |
| "in the event that" | "if" |
| "subsequent to" | "after" |
| "at this point in time" | "now" |
| "shall" (in non-binding UI/marketing copy) | "will" |

The carve-out: a defined term of art stays exactly as-is when the term itself is doing
real work — when swapping it for a plain-language paraphrase would change or blur its
technical or legal meaning. **Personal data** (in a privacy policy) is the clearest
example: it's not just "your info," because the term has a specific regulatory definition
and specific obligations that the plain paraphrase doesn't carry. Other terms in this
category: *service-level agreement*, *sub-processor*, *chargeback*. The test is simple —
would a specialist wince at the substitution because something real got lost? If yes,
keep the term of art and, where it's the reader's first encounter with it, define it in
plain language alongside it rather than replacing it.

- **Do (unnecessary jargon, simplified):** "Let us know before Friday if anything
  changes."
- **Don't:** "Please advise our team prior to the end of the business week in the event
  that any of the aforementioned circumstances should change."
- **Do (necessary precision, kept):** "We process your personal data only to provide the
  service, as described in our privacy policy."
- **Don't (over-simplified, loses meaning):** "We just use your info to help you out."

## Active voice over passive

Active voice names who's doing the action, which is almost always what the reader needs
to know. Passive voice hides the actor — sometimes intentionally, but usually just out of
habit — and forces the reader to guess or re-read.

- **Do:** "We processed your refund on July 18."
- **Don't:** "Your refund was processed on July 18."

The passive version isn't wrong, but it leaves open *who* processed it, which is important
if something later goes wrong with the refund. Default to active; use passive only when the
actor is genuinely unknown or truly irrelevant to the reader (e.g., "the account was
created in 2019" when authorship of the account isn't the point).

## Second person: address the reader as "you"

Write to the person reading, not about them in the third person and not in the abstract
passive. This is consistent with the *Human* voice attribute, but it's a mechanical
habit worth calling out on its own.

- **Do:** "You'll receive an invoice on the first of each month."
- **Don't:** "Customers will receive an invoice on the first of each month." *(when
  writing directly to that customer)*

## One idea per sentence

A sentence carrying two or three ideas asks the reader to hold all of them in mind at
once before any of them resolve. Split compound sentences at the seams — most of the
time, two short sentences are easier to read than one long one joined with "and" or a
semicolon.

- **Do:** "We received your signed contract. Your project is now open, and you'll get a
  welcome email with your account login within one business day."
- **Don't:** "We received your signed contract, and as a result your project is now open,
  which means you will shortly receive a welcome email containing your account login
  details, and this should arrive within one business day."

## Sentence case for UI text

Buttons, headings, and nav labels use sentence case — capitalize only the first word and
proper nouns — rather than Title Case. This matches the convention set by GOV.UK and
Polaris, and it reads as calmer and less shouty at small sizes, where every word starting
with a capital letter starts to look like a wall of emphasis.

| Sentence case (use this) | Title Case (don't) |
| --- | --- |
| "Save changes" | "Save Changes" |
| "New project" | "New Project" |
| "Billing and invoices" | "Billing And Invoices" |

The one exception is a proper noun that's always capitalized regardless of position —
"Acme," a person's name, a defined product name.

## The read-it-aloud test

Before shipping a sentence, say it out loud as if a customer were sitting across the desk.
If it would sound stiff, robotic, or over-formal spoken aloud to a real person, rewrite
it until it wouldn't. Nobody says "please be advised that the aforementioned document has
been received" to a customer in person — so it shouldn't be written that way either. This
is the fastest gut-check available and it catches almost everything the rules above are
trying to prevent.
