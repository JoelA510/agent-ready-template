# UI Copy Patterns

This is likely the most-consulted page in the whole system — not the highest-concept, just
the one people reach for daily. Building a delete confirmation, an error toast, an empty
list? The pattern is probably here already, in the shape Shopify's Polaris popularized:
a short rule plus real, copy-pasteable examples, rather than an abstract principle you have
to translate yourself every time. Six situations come up in almost every product surface
this system covers; this page gives each one a fixed shape.

Like **Writing Principles**, these patterns are settled defaults meant to be used as-is,
not reopened per feature — consistency here is worth more than any individual writer's
preference for how to phrase a single button.

## Button labels

**Pattern: verb + noun, sentence case, specific enough to act on without reading anything
else on the screen.** A button is often read in isolation — scanned in a row of other
buttons, or announced alone by a screen reader — so it has to carry its own meaning. A
bare verb with no object forces the reader to go find context that a few extra words
would have supplied for free.

| Don't (bare or vague) | Do (verb + specific noun) |
| --- | --- |
| "Save" | "Save changes" |
| "Delete" | "Delete project" |
| "Submit" | "Send invoice" |
| "OK" | "Confirm and continue" |
| "Yes" | "Delete project" *(name the actual action, not just an answer to an implied question)* |

Never ship a bare "OK," "Yes," or "Submit" on anything consequential — deleting,
canceling, or committing money or a subscription change. Reserve terse one-word buttons
for truly low-stakes, fully reversible actions where a longer label would be needless
noise, and even then prefer a specific verb ("Skip," "Dismiss") over a content-free one.

## Error messages

**Pattern: what happened, why if it's known, and how to fix it — in that order, never
blaming the person reading it.** An error is the system's problem to explain clearly, not
a chance to scold the user for something the interface should have prevented or clarified
up front. A raw system error or stack trace surfaced verbatim tells the reader nothing
they can act on.

- **Do:** "Enter a valid email address, like name@example.com."
- **Don't:** "Error: invalid input."


- **Do:** "We couldn't save this document. Check your connection and try again."
- **Don't:** "Something went wrong." *(too vague — nothing to act on)*


- **Do:** "This file is too large to upload. Files must be under 25 MB."
- **Don't:** "Upload failed: ERR_FILE_SIZE_EXCEEDED_413"

If the underlying cause genuinely isn't knowable (a transient network blip, a third-party
outage), it's fine to say so plainly and give the one thing the reader can still do — "We
couldn't reach the server. Try again in a moment" — rather than inventing a reason that
isn't true.

## Empty states

**Pattern: say what this space is for, then give the one primary action that fills it.**
An empty list or dashboard with no copy reads as broken, not empty-on-purpose. The fix
isn't decoration — it's telling the reader what normally lives here and how to put the
first thing in it.

- **Do (a projects list with nothing in it yet):** "No projects yet. Create your first
  project to start tracking tasks, deadlines, and billing in one place."
- **Don't:** "No data." *(reads as an error, gives no next step)*


- **Do (an invoices tab with none sent):** "No invoices sent yet. Create an invoice to
  bill a customer for work or expenses on this project."
- **Don't:** "Empty."

Always pair the explanation with the actual button or link that resolves it — "Create
your first project" should sit right next to (or be) the action, not just describe it in
prose while the real button is labeled something generic elsewhere on the screen.

## Confirmation dialogs

**Pattern: state the specific, concrete consequence of the action — never a bare "Are you
sure?"** A generic confirmation forces the reader to remember what they were about to do
and guess at the blast radius. Naming the actual consequence lets them make the decision
with the information already in front of them, without hunting for it.

- **Do:** "Delete this project? This removes all associated documents and can't be
  undone."
- **Don't:** "Are you sure?"


- **Do:** "Discard unsaved changes? Your edits to this document will be lost."
- **Don't:** "Are you sure you want to leave this page?"


- **Do:** "Sign out of Acme? You'll need to sign back in to view your projects."
- **Don't:** "Confirm sign out"

If the action is destructive and irreversible, say so in plain words ("can't be undone,"
"permanently removes") rather than leaving reversibility ambiguous — that ambiguity is
exactly what makes a reader hesitate and re-read the dialog three times, or worse, click
through without understanding what they agreed to.

## Form field help text

**Pattern: add help text only when the label alone isn't enough, and use it to explain
*why* the information is needed or *what format* is expected.** Help text under every
field, restating the label in longer words, trains readers to stop reading it. Reserve it
for fields where the label genuinely leaves a real question open.

- **Do (format unclear from the label alone):** Label: "Tax ID." Help text: "Enter your
  tax ID as it appears on your registration documents."
- **Don't:** Label: "Tax ID." Help text: "Enter your tax ID." *(adds nothing the label
  didn't already say)*


- **Do (why it's needed isn't obvious):** Label: "Phone number." Help text: "We'll only
  use this to reach you about urgent account updates."
- **Don't:** No help text on a field asking for a tax ID with no explanation of why a
  billing form needs it.

If a label is already fully self-explanatory ("Email address," "First name"), skip help
text entirely — an empty field with no clutter under it is a better outcome than help
text invented to fill the space.

## Success messages

**Pattern: short, past tense, and specific about what happened.** A success message
confirms that a specific thing occurred, not that something vaguely good happened
somewhere. Specificity here also doubles as a receipt the reader can act on later — "sent
to jane@example.com" is something they can point back to if a customer says they never
received it.

- **Do:** "Project created."
- **Don't:** "Success!"


- **Do:** "Invoice sent to jane@example.com."
- **Don't:** "Sent!"


- **Do:** "Changes saved."
- **Don't:** "Great job! Everything is saved and looking good."

## Quick reference

| Situation | Structure | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Button label | Verb + specific noun, sentence case | "Delete project" |
| Error message | What happened + why (if known) + how to fix | "Enter a valid email address, like name@example.com." |
| Empty state | What this space is for + the primary action | "No projects yet. Create your first project to start tracking tasks, deadlines, and billing in one place." |
| Confirmation dialog | The specific, concrete consequence | "Delete this project? This removes all associated documents and can't be undone." |
| Form field help text | Why it's needed, or what format is expected | "Enter your tax ID as it appears on your registration documents." |
| Success message | Short, past tense, specific | "Invoice sent to jane@example.com." |
